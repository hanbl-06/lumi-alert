The invisible line item

Nobody knows a light is out until someone complains.

A failed fixture is quiet. It doesn't trip an alarm, it doesn't open a ticket, and it keeps drawing standby current while it fails. So detection gets outsourced to the two worst sensors available: a night patrol driving every street, or a resident who finally picks up the phone.

Both are slow. Both are expensive. Both send a truck out with a guess instead of an address.

Patrols cost more than parts

Driving a grid to look for dark lamps is paid labor, fuel and vehicle hours spent confirming that almost everything is fine.

Dark weeks become liability

Unlit walkways, lots and stairwells are the first thing cited after an incident — and the hardest thing to prove you addressed quickly.

Blind truck rolls repeat

Without a fault code, a crew arrives, diagnoses on a ladder, leaves for the right driver or lamp, and comes back. One failure, two visits.

How it works

Three parts. One answer: which lamp, and what's wrong with it.

No new conduit, no controls retrofit, no rip-and-replace. The sensor sits inline with the fixture you already own.

01

Sense

A sealed inline module reads current draw, voltage, lumen output and temperature at the fixture. It knows the difference between off by schedule, dimming toward failure, flickering driver and dead lamp.

02

Report

Each module meshes to its neighbours and backhauls over LTE-M or LoRaWAN — so a single gateway covers a corridor, a campus or a lot. Battery-backed, so a power loss is itself a reported event.

03

Resolve

A dark lamp becomes a work order with a pole ID, a GPS pin, a fault code and the part to bring. Route the whole night's failures in one pass; track mean time to repair instead of guessing at it.
